Normal forms at equilibrium points and periodic orbits

A gentle introduction

Claudio Vestini
Group meeting, 23 November 2026

Two weeks ago I ended on normal forms as the next talk. Today has three parts: what a normal form is, on a pendulum, then what I reproduced in DyLAN from the papers of Jorba's group, at an equilibrium point and at periodic orbits, and last one experiment of my own, a Gaussian cloud at an L1 Lyapunov orbit.

Motivation and outline

  1. What is a normal form?
    • Take a nonlinear vector field \(\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t) = \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}(t), t)\). “Simplify” \(\boldsymbol{f}\) by means of near-identity (i.e. \(\vb x = \vb y + \vb P(\vb y)\)) coordinate transformations. What is left afterwards is the normal form.
  2. Why is it useful?
    • It enables you to describe the dynamics around an invariant object analytically.

Some basics

    Further motivation: linear stability

    Near-equilibrium dynamics:

    \[\delta\dot{\vb x} = \vb A\,\delta\vb x, \qquad \vb A = \vb D\vb f(\vb x_{\mathcal{L}_1}).\]

    The eigenspaces of \(\vb A\) at \(\mathcal{L}_1\):

    • \(\lambda<0\): stable eigendirection \(E^s\),
    • \(\lambda>0\): unstable eigendirection \(E^u\),
    • \(\pm \mathrm{i}\omega_{xy}\): planar center \(E^c_{xy}\).

    By \(z \to -z\) symmetry, vertical decouples:

    \[\vb A = \begin{pmatrix} \vb A_{xy} & \vb 0 \\ \vb 0 & \class{tone-blue}{\vb A_z} \end{pmatrix} \;\Longrightarrow\; \pm \mathrm{i}\omega_z\!: \; \class{tone-blue}{E^c_z}.\]

    Step 0: This is the appendix slide of two weeks ago, slowed down, and it is where the normal forms start.

    Step 1: Near an equilibrium point, the flow is its Jacobian A, the derivative of f at the point. The plot is the planar linear flow at L1 of the Earth–Moon CR3BP, in km, with the Earth to the left. The state has four components, x, y, and their velocities, and the arrows are drawn on the plane of the two real modes, which projects one to one onto the positions. Each arrow is the velocity of a deviation at its point.

    Step 2: The solution of a linear system is a sum of eigenmodes. Along an eigenvector, A only scales, hence the deviation grows or decays as e to the lambda t and keeps its direction. At L1 the Jacobian has the real pair ±2.932. The violet line is the stable eigenvector, projected onto x and y: the arrows along it point into L1.

    Step 3: The orange line is the unstable eigenvector, and the arrows along it point away.

    Step 4: These two lines are the linear stable and unstable manifolds of L1.

    Step 5: Every other deviation is a sum of the two modes. The stable part dies out and the unstable part takes over, hence it comes in along the violet line and leaves along the orange one, on a hyperbola. The clock runs 1.4 time units, about 6 days, over which the unstable mode grows by e to the 4.1, about 60.

    Step 6: The imaginary planar pair ±i ω_xy (at Earth–Moon L1, ω_xy = 2.334 in dimensionless time) spans the planar center eigenspace E^c_xy. Its arrows turn around L1 without growing or decaying.

    Step 7: Its orbit is the dashed blue ellipse, three and a half times taller than wide — the linear Lyapunov orbit that the planar Lyapunov family starts from.

    Step 8: Now tilt into 3D: by z -> -z reflection symmetry of the CR3BP, the Jacobian A is block-diagonal — zero coupling between the 4D planar block A_xy and the 2D vertical block A_z. The vertical direction decouples as an independent harmonic center E^c_z with ±i ω_z (at Earth–Moon L1, ω_z = sqrt(c_2) = 2.269). So L1 is saddle x center in the plane, times an uncoupled vertical center — and this decoupling holds at all five Lagrange points for every mu.

    Step 9: Can we do better than linear? That leads straight to what happens nonlinearly at all five Lagrange points.

    Step 0: Start with the 4D planar + 2D vertical split. By z -> -z reflection symmetry, the vertical direction decouples linearly at all five Lagrange points and for every mass ratio mu as a pure harmonic center (shown oscillating calmly in blue on the right). All linear bifurcations live in the 4D horizontal plane. Step 1 (L1, L2, L3, every mu): Planar saddle x center (+/- lambda_1 real, +/- i omega_2 imaginary), vertical center (+/- i sqrt(c_2), with sqrt(c_2) > 1). On the right, stable E^s in violet, unstable E^u in orange, and both centers in blue. Step 2 (L4, L5, mu < mu_R): All six eigenvalues lie on the imaginary axis (planar short-period omega_1 and long-period omega_2 centers, plus vertical omega_z = 1 identically). The mu-axis appears below as stability now depends on the mass ratio mu. Linear theory gives no nonlinear verdict here. Step 3 (L4, L5, mu = mu_R = 0.03852, Gascheau 1843 / Routh 1875): Krein collision at +/- i/sqrt(2) creates a non-diagonalizable 1:1 Jordan block with linear secular growth ~ t in the plane. mu_R marks onto the axis below; vertical +/- i is still untouched. Step 4 (L4, L5, mu > mu_R): The planar quadruple splits off the imaginary axis into a complex saddle (+/- alpha +/- i beta), spiraling out exponentially in orange and in along violet. The unstable regime past mu_R shades on the mu-line; even here, vertical +/- i stays a pure center. Step 5 (Planar, nonlinear): In 2 DOF, the planar nonlinear problem is completely closed: L1-L3 and mu > mu_R are unstable; L4, L5 are Lyapunov stable for every mu <= mu_R (Deprit & Deprit-Bartholomé 1967 via Arnold; Meyer & Schmidt 1986 at D_4 = 0 from degree 6) except the two resonant values 1:3 (0.01352) and 1:2 (0.02429) (Markeev 1969, Alfriend 1970). On the right: why — in 2 DOF an energy level is 3D, so KAM 2-tori wall orbits in. Step 6 (Spatial, nonlinear — L1, L2, L3): Reveal the Spatial, nonlinear column starting with L1–L3: the real saddle persists in 3D, so all three collinear points remain unstable. Step 7 (Spatial, nonlinear — L4, L5, mu < mu_R): Here is the surprise — Lyapunov stability is still OPEN (outside the same two unstable resonances)! On the right: in 3 DOF an energy level is 5D, so 3-tori separate nothing (leaving room for Arnold drift), and H_2 = omega_1 I_1 - omega_2 I_2 + I_3 is indefinite. Below: what IS proved on the violet intervals (0, mu_1), (mu_2, mu_(1,2,0)), and (mu_(1,2,0), mu_R) — formal stability to all orders and Nekhoroshev exponent 1/3 (directional quasi-convexity; Benettin–Fassò–Guzzo 1998; Cárcamo-Díaz et al. 2020). Step 8 (The (mu_1, mu_2) gap + Earth–Moon): Inside (mu_1, mu_2) = (0.01091, 0.01638) directional quasi-convexity fails (only 3-jet non-degenerate), formal stability holds only outside a countable dense set, and the Nekhoroshev exponent drops from 1/3 to 1/20 ("rather poor"). Earth–Moon (mu = 0.01215) sits squarely inside this gap! Step 9 (Spatial, nonlinear — L4, L5, mu = mu_R): At Routh's critical mass ratio mu_R = 0.03852, spatial L4, L5 are formally stable (marked under mu_R on the axis). Step 10 (Spatial, nonlinear — L4, L5, mu > mu_R + punchline): Above mu_R, the complex saddle makes L4, L5 linearly and nonlinearly unstable (orange band past mu_R). Every single nonlinear entry on this slide — degree 4 & 6 planar proofs, 1:2 and 1:3 resonant instability, and high-order exponential-time effective stability bounds at Earth–Moon L5 — is a normal-form computation. For questions: - Fayad 2023 (JAMS) built analytic Hamiltonians with a Lyapunov-unstable elliptic point, needing >= 4 DOF for arbitrary frequencies and explicitly noting 3 DOF with Diophantine frequencies remains open (and none is the CR3BP). - Do not cite Sosnitskii 2025 (arXiv:2510.13388): unrefereed, and its proof contradicts the purely imaginary eigenvalues. - Arnold diffusion near L4, L5: neither proved nor observed numerically in the spatial CR3BP. - Terminology: Jorba's frequency-conditioned exponential time bound is called "effective stability" in the literature, reserving "Nekhoroshev" for bounds without frequency conditions.

    How much of the nonlinearity is real?

    Near an equilibrium point, we expand the Hamiltonian by degree,

    \[H = \class{lin}{H_2} + \class{non}{H_3 + H_4 + H_5 + \dots}\]
    • The flow of \(H_2\) is linear, and we know it in closed form.
    • The terms \(H_3, H_4, \dots\) depend on the coordinates we chose.

    A canonical change of coordinates removes most of them,

    \[\bar H = H_2 + \bar H_3 + \bar H_4 + \dots + \bar H_N + \text{remainder}.\]

    What we can remove was the coordinates. What stays is the dynamics.

    Step 0: This is the one question of the talk. Near an equilibrium point, and later near a periodic orbit, we expand the Hamiltonian in powers of the distance from it. H_d collects the terms of degree d.

    Step 1: H_2 gives the linear flow of the last talk: the saddle and the two centers at L1. Its solution is exponentials and rotations.

    Step 2: Everything from degree 3 on is the nonlinearity. But these terms are not a property of the system alone. They change when we change coordinates.

    Step 3: A normal form is the simplest Hamiltonian we can reach with a canonical change of coordinates close to the identity. H-bar_d holds only the terms of degree d that no change can remove. We stop at a maximum degree N, and what is left beyond it is the remainder.

    Step 4: So the terms we removed were an artifact of the coordinates, and the terms that stay are real: they change the dynamics, for example the period of an orbit with its amplitude.

    Why compute a normal form

    1. An integrable local model. The actions are constant and the angles linear in time, hence a state is propagated by evaluation.
    2. The orbits near the point. On the center manifold only 2 degrees of freedom are left, and Poincaré sections show the Lyapunov, halo, Lissajous, and quasi-halo orbits.
    3. Bounds on stability times. The actions are integrals up to the remainder, whose size bounds how slowly an orbit can drift.

    Step 1: Three uses. First, the normal form is integrable. In its coordinates, a trajectory is written down for any time without integrating. The cost is in the change of coordinates, not in the flow.

    Step 2: Second, if we only remove the terms that couple the saddle to the centers, the unstable direction can be switched off. What is left is a Hamiltonian with 2 degrees of freedom, and a Poincaré section of it is a picture of every bounded orbit near the point. This is the paper of Jorba and Masdemont.

    Step 3: Third, near a stable point or orbit, the actions of the normal form are constant up to the remainder. The size of the remainder then bounds the speed of any drift, which gives stability over long but finite times. Jorba and Villanueva do this near L5.

    One change of coordinates per degree

    The flow at time 1 of a polynomial \(G_d\) is a canonical change,

    \[\vb z = \vb w + \vb J\,\nabla G_d(\vb w) + \dots\]

    In the new coordinates, the Hamiltonian is a Lie series,

    \[\hat H = H + \{H, G_d\} + \tfrac{1}{2}\{\{H, G_d\}, G_d\} + \dots\]

    Below degree \(d\), the degree of \(G_d\), nothing changes, and at degree \(d\)

    \[\hat H_d = H_d + \{H_2, \class{gen}{G_d}\}.\]

    We choose the generating function \(G_d\) to cancel as much of \(H_d\) as we can. This is the homological equation.

    Step 0: How do we change coordinates and stay Hamiltonian? We do not write the change. We write one scalar polynomial G_d, and take as the change the flow of G_d for one unit of time. A flow of a Hamiltonian is canonical, so the new system is Hamiltonian for free. z are the old coordinates, w the new ones, and J is the symplectic matrix.

    Step 1: The new Hamiltonian is the old one composed with this flow, and it expands as a series of nested Poisson brackets. A computer only needs products and derivatives of polynomials for this, and never has to invert a series.

    Step 2: A bracket of degrees a and b has degree a + b - 2. So with G of degree d, the lowest new term is the bracket of H_2 with G_d, of degree d. The degrees below d are untouched: what we cleaned before stays clean.

    Step 3: The only unknown is G_d, and it enters linearly. We choose it to cancel as much as possible of H_d. This linear equation is the homological equation, and its solution is one division per monomial, as on the next slide. Then we go on to degree d + 1, with a new generating function.

    A monomial is removed by dividing its coefficient by its divisor. A resonant monomial has a zero divisor, and it stays.

    For a Hamiltonian, the monomials with \(\vb k_q = \vb k_p\) are always resonant, hence \(\bar H\) is a function of the products \(q_j p_j\).

    Step 0: Those of you who took MAE 541 saw normal forms for a vector field, from Guckenheimer and Holmes. This is the same computation. There, the linear part is a diagonal matrix Lambda, the change is x = y + P(y) with one polynomial per component, and the monomial y to the a in component i is removed by dividing by the sum of a_j lambda_j minus lambda_i. Jorba does the Hamiltonian version. The linear part is H_2, a sum of eta_j q_j p_j in complex coordinates, the change is the flow of one polynomial, and the monomial q to the k_q times p to the k_p is an eigenvector of the bracket with H_2, with the eigenvalue k_p minus k_q dotted with eta.

    Step 1: In both, the homological equation is diagonal in the monomials. Solving it is one division per monomial. If the divisor is zero, the monomial is resonant and cannot be removed.

    Step 2: The difference is this. For a general vector field, resonances are accidents of the eigenvalues. A Hamiltonian has its eigenvalues in pairs plus and minus eta, so the monomials with equal powers of q_j and p_j are always resonant. These are the powers of the products q_j p_j, which are the actions. So the normal form of a Hamiltonian is a function of the actions alone, if there is no other resonance.

    Larger swings are slower. Is this real, or the coordinates?

    Step 0: One worked example, with one degree of freedom, so that every step fits on a slide. A pendulum of unit length and mass: q is the angle from the vertical and p its momentum.

    Step 1: Expand the cosine at the rest point. The constant does nothing. H_2 is a harmonic oscillator with frequency 1, and the first nonlinear term is H_4. There is no H_3.

    Step 2: The natural coordinates of H_2 are its action and angle. I is half the squared radius in the phase plane, and phi is the polar angle, measured so that it grows in time.

    Step 3: In these, H_2 is just I: the angle turns at rate 1 for every amplitude. And H_4 is minus one sixth of I squared times the fourth power of sin phi. It depends on the angle, so the action is no longer constant, and the orbits are not circles.

    The average stays, the harmonics are removed

    \[H_4 = \class{avg}{-\tfrac{I^2}{16}} + \class{osc}{\tfrac{I^2}{12}\cos 2\varphi - \tfrac{I^2}{48}\cos 4\varphi}\]

    Since \(\{H_2, G_4\} = -\partial G_4 / \partial\varphi\), the divisor of the harmonic \(k\) is \(k\),

    \[G_4 = \tfrac{I^2}{12 \cdot 2}\sin 2\varphi - \tfrac{I^2}{48 \cdot 4}\sin 4\varphi.\]

    The average is resonant: it stays,

    \[\bar H = I - \tfrac{1}{16}I^2, \qquad \omega(I) = 1 - \tfrac{1}{8}I.\]

    In the new coordinates the orbits are circles, turned at a rate set by the radius alone.

    Step 0: Write the fourth power of the sine as a Fourier series in the angle. H_4 is an average, minus I squared over 16, plus two harmonics, at twice and four times the angle.

    Step 1: The homological equation is simple here. H_2 is I, so the bracket of H_2 with G is minus the derivative of G in the angle. To cancel a cosine of k phi, G needs a sine of k phi divided by k. The divisors are 2 and 4: k times the frequency, which is 1. These two terms were the coordinates.

    Step 2: The average does not depend on the angle. A derivative in the angle can never produce it: its divisor is zero. It is the resonant term, and it stays. So the normal form to degree 4 is I minus I squared over 16, and the frequency is its derivative, 1 minus I over 8. With I equal to half the squared amplitude, this is the textbook period of the pendulum, 2 pi times 1 plus the amplitude squared over 16. The slowing of the pendulum is real. The wobble of the orbits was the coordinates.

    Step 3: The dashed curves are the four orbits of the last slide. The solid ones are the same orbits after the change generated by G_4. They are circles, and each dot turns at a constant rate that depends only on its radius. Normal form theory for one degree of freedom is averaging over the angle. In Jorba's complex coordinates, the monomial z to the a times z-bar to the b is the harmonic a minus b, so "remove the harmonics" is his "remove every monomial with k_q different from k_p".

    How far does it hold?

    At higher degrees,

    \[\bar H = I - \tfrac{I^2}{16} - \tfrac{I^3}{256} - \tfrac{5 I^4}{8192} - \dots\]
    • \(N = 4\) is right for small swings.
    • Each degree extends the range.
    • No polynomial reaches \(\omega = 0\) at the separatrix.

    A normal form is local. We choose \(N\) for the neighborhood we need.

    Step 0: We can go on: degree 6, degree 8, each with its own G. Each adds one power of the action. The white curve is the exact frequency of the pendulum against its action, from the elliptic integral.

    Step 1: Degree 4 is the straight line 1 minus I over 8. It is tangent to the truth at the origin.

    Step 2: Degrees 6 and 8 follow the truth further out.

    Step 3: But at the separatrix, where the pendulum just reaches the top, the period is infinite and the frequency is zero. A polynomial in I cannot do this. The separatrix is at an action of 8 over pi.

    Step 4: This is the limit of every normal form in this talk. It is a local model around a point or an orbit, and its domain is bounded by the nearest thing it cannot describe. For questions: for the pendulum the series converges inside the separatrix. In general, with two or more frequencies, small divisors make the series diverge, and there is a best degree for each distance. This will come back in the Gaussian part, where part of the cloud is outside the domain.

    At a collinear point, \(\vb\eta = (\lambda_1, i\omega_2, i\omega_3)\): one saddle pair \((q_1, p_1)\) and two centers.

    Step 0: Back to three degrees of freedom. After a linear change, H_2 is the sum of eta_j q_j p_j. We are free to choose how much to remove. In the full normal form we remove everything we can, every monomial with different powers of q and p. The result depends on the three products only. In the center manifold reduction we remove less: only the monomials with different powers of q_1 and p_1, the saddle pair. The result depends on the saddle pair through its product, and on the center variables in any way.

    Step 1: Why remove less? The divisors. In the full normal form, a divisor is a combination of the two center frequencies with integers, and it can be close to zero. In the reduction, every divisor has a real part that is a nonzero multiple of lambda_1, so it is never small. The reduction holds in a larger neighborhood.

    Step 2: The full normal form gives three integrals and the flow in closed form. The reduction gives an invariant set: if q_1 and p_1 are zero, they stay zero. This set is the center manifold, and on it we have a Hamiltonian with 2 degrees of freedom, H_cm.

    Step 3: At L1, L2, and L3, eta is one real number and two imaginary ones. This is the saddle times center times center of the recap slide.

    The recipe that a computer follows

    1. Move the origin to the point, and put \(H_2\) in the form \(\sum_j \eta_j\, q_j p_j\) with a linear symplectic change.
    2. Expand \(H\) up to degree \(N\), directly in these coordinates.
    3. For \(d = 3, \dots, N\): divide the coefficients to remove in \(H_d\) by their divisors to get \(G_d\), then transform \(H\) with the Lie series.
    4. Transform the coordinates with the same \(G_d\), to go from a state to the normal form and back.

    Every object is a polynomial stored by its coefficients: 74 613 of them up to degree 16 in 6 variables.

    Step 1: The whole algorithm, as in Jorba's 1999 paper and as in DyLAN. First the linear algebra: the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the linearization give a symplectic change that diagonalizes H_2, in complex coordinates.

    Step 2: Then the expansion. For the CR3BP the potential is expanded with a recurrence of Legendre polynomials, and the linear change is substituted into the recurrence, so the expansion is produced directly in the diagonal coordinates.

    Step 3: Then the loop over degrees. One division per coefficient gives G_d, and nested Poisson brackets give the new Hamiltonian.

    Step 4: The same generating functions transform the coordinates. This is what we evaluate to bring a state into the normal form and to bring it back.

    Step 5: Nothing here is symbolic algebra in the usual sense. A polynomial is an array of coefficients, and the cost is the number of monomials, which grows fast with the degree.

      Each of these results is proved with a normal form.

      Step 0: Before the results, what is known about the five points. The collinear points have a saddle direction for every mass ratio, hence they are unstable, and this is why we reduce to the center manifold there. The triangular points are linearly stable below the mass ratio of Routh, about 0.0385 (Gascheau 1843, Routh 1875). In the planar problem the question is closed. They are stable for every mass ratio below Routh's, apart from two values, 0.0243 and 0.0135, where the frequencies are in a 1:2 and a 1:3 resonance and the point is unstable (Deprit and Deprit-Bartholomé 1967, Markeev 1969, Alfriend 1970, Meyer and Schmidt 1986). In the spatial problem nobody has proved stability, and nobody has proved instability, apart from the same two values.

      Step 1: The reason is a count of dimensions. With two degrees of freedom, an energy level has three dimensions, and a torus of two dimensions splits it into an inside and an outside. An orbit between two tori is trapped. With three degrees of freedom, the level has five dimensions and the tori have three, hence they do not separate it. The energy does not help either, since the quadratic part has frequencies of both signs.

      Step 2: What is proved in space is weaker. Most orbits near the point lie on invariant tori (Markeev 1971). For all but a countable set of mass ratios, the point is stable to every order of the normal form (Cárcamo-Díaz, Palacián, Vidal, Yanguas 2020). And an orbit that starts at a distance r stays close for a time that grows as an exponential of a power of 1/r (Benettin, Fassò, Guzzo 1998). For questions: Fayad built in 2023 analytic Hamiltonians with an unstable elliptic point of this kind, hence stability is not automatic, but his examples are not the three-body problem.

      Step 3: Every row of this table is a normal form computation. Degree 2 for the collinear points, degree 4 and 6 for the planar result, a resonant normal form at the two exceptions, and a high degree for the exponential times. This last one is what Jorba computes at L5, and what we reproduce next.

            The pendulum again: \(\mathcal{L}_3\) is the saddle

            Along the orbit of the small primary, the motion is a pendulum.

            • \(\mathcal{L}_4\) and \(\mathcal{L}_5\) are its centers.
            • \(\mathcal{L}_3\) is its saddle.

            The manifolds of \(\mathcal{L}_3\) are what is left of the separatrix.

            A normal form at \(\mathcal{L}_5\) holds well inside them. They are conjectured to bound the stable region.

            Step 0: Remember the pendulum of the theory part. A body that shares the orbit of the small primary is a pendulum in its angle from this primary. L4 and L5 are the two rest positions, at 60 degrees ahead and behind. L3, on the far side of the large primary, is the unstable position, the pendulum standing upright.

            Step 1: These are the unstable and the stable manifold of L3 for the Sun and Jupiter, integrated in the planar problem. Each branch leaves L3, turns around L4 or L5 before it reaches Jupiter, and comes back. The two nearly lie on each other. They do not close: the gap is exponentially small in the square root of the mass ratio (Baldomá, Giralt, Guardia 2022 and 2023). Inside a loop, orbits turn around one triangular point. Outside, they are horseshoe orbits.

            Step 2: A normal form at L5 is a polynomial around the center, as for the pendulum, and it cannot describe the separatrix. For the Earth and the Moon, the normal form of degree 16 holds in a core of about a hundredth of the Earth–Moon distance in the plane, while the stable region is far larger. Gómez, Jorba, Masdemont and Simó conjectured in 2001 that the edge of the stable region is made of the stable and unstable manifolds of the center manifold of L3. They computed it in the planar problem only.

            Step 3: For the Earth and the Moon the mass ratio is too large for this picture. The manifolds of L3 no longer turn back before the Moon. They pass by it, and one branch goes on to the inside and the other to the outside. This holds above a mass ratio of 0.0117 (Barrabés and Ollé 2006), and the Earth and the Moon are at 0.0122.
